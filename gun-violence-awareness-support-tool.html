Pause. You are not alone, and you have a choice.

Having angry or dark thoughts does not make you a bad person. It means you are hurting and something needs to change. What matters is what you do next.

Strong anger and pain rise fast, but they do come down. Hurting someone cannot be undone. Asking for help can be. Here is a simple plan for right now:

1. Step awayLeave the room or the situation. Put distance between you and anything that could hurt someone. Do the 60 second breathing below.
2. Tell one personA parent, relative, friend, teacher, pastor, coach or counsellor. Say: "I am in a bad place and I need help right now."
3. Get supportTalk to a helpline. They listen without judging, and it is free. If you feel you might act on a thought, call emergency services now.

Why reaching out is the stronger choice: it protects other people, it protects your future, and it protects the people who love you. Many people who ask for help are met with care, not punishment.

Worried about someone else?

Trust your gut. You do not need to be sure to speak up. Acting early can prevent harm and help someone get support.

Things worth taking seriously:

  • Talking about wanting to hurt others, or about having a weapon to do it
  • Sudden anger, obsession with revenge, or pulling away from everyone
  • Saying goodbye, giving things away, or "they will all be sorry" type comments

Is there a weapon, a threat, or a plan to hurt someone?

Call your local emergency number now.

Do not confront the person or try to handle this alone. Tell the dispatcher what you know: who, where, what weapon, and what was said. Then tell a trusted adult.

When in doubt, treat it as a yes. It is always okay to call emergency services or tell a trusted adult.

What to say

  • Say what you have noticed: "I am worried about you. I care about you."
  • Ask an open question, like "What has been going on?", then listen without arguing.

What to avoid

Do not argue, shame or joke about it. Do not promise to keep it secret if someone could get hurt.

Who to tell

A trusted adult, school staff, a faith leader, a family member, or a helpline.

Words you can copy and send

I have noticed you seem really low and angry lately. I care about you. Can we talk?
I am not here to judge you. I am worried about what you said. Who could we talk to together?
I do not want to keep this secret, because I am scared someone could get hurt. I am going to ask a trusted adult for help.

Telling someone is not betrayal. It can be the thing that keeps everyone safe, including the person you are worried about.

When violence in the news is getting to you

Feeling anxious, angry, numb or on edge after violence is a normal response. It does not mean you are weak.

  • Choose when you check the news, and turn off alerts. Try not to scroll before bed.
  • Look away from graphic images and videos. Seeing them again and again can raise your stress.
  • Keep your routines: sleep, food, movement and time with people you trust.
  • Do one small thing for someone else. It can ease the helpless feeling.
  • If a child is worried, ask what they have heard and listen first.
  • Use simple, honest words. Tell them the adults are working to keep them safe.
  • Keep the news off around young children, and watch for changes in sleep, appetite or mood.

If the distress stays strong for weeks, or it affects your sleep, eating or work, please talk to a doctor or counsellor.

Making things safer at home

This is for anyone who lives with a firearm, or knows one is nearby. It is about safety, not blame.

  • The safest choice is no gun in the home. If there is one, store it unloaded and locked.
  • Lock ammunition away separately from the gun.
  • Keep keys and codes away from children, and away from anyone who is in crisis.
  • If someone at home is angry, in crisis or thinking of suicide, ask a trusted friend or relative to hold the firearm for a while. Local rules differ, so check what is allowed where you live.
  • Teach children never to touch a gun, and to tell an adult at once if they find one.

In many places, a large share of gun deaths are suicides. Creating distance between a person in crisis and a firearm can save a life.

    For teens: why asking for help beats the alternative

    Being a teenager can be really hard. Pressure, bullying, anger and feeling invisible are real. You deserve support, and you are not weak for needing it.

    Feelings pass. Actions do not.The worst moment feels like it will last forever, but it does not. Hurting someone stays forever, for them, for you and for both families.
    Asking for help is a power move.It takes more courage to say "I am struggling" than to act out of anger. You stay in control of your own life.
    You protect your future.Your goals, your friends and your freedom are all still ahead of you. One violent act can end all of it.
    Adults usually respond with care.Many people want to help once they know. You do not need the perfect words. You just need to start.
    You are not the only one.Many young people carry dark thoughts and anger. Telling someone is how it gets lighter.

    Need words? Copy one and send it:

    I am not okay and I need to talk. Can you stay with me for a bit?
    I have been having really angry thoughts and I need help. Please do not judge me.
    Something is going on and I am scared of what I might do. Can you help me get support?

    Who you can tell today: a parent or guardian, an older sibling or cousin, a teacher, a school counsellor, a coach, a pastor or imam, or a helpline.

    If you are in danger, or think you might hurt someone, call emergency services now. If you know someone is planning to hurt others, tell a trusted adult straight away. That could save lives.
